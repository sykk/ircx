ircx / live channel studies

A busy channel, and the protocol that makes it readable.

Continuing the readability studies. The verdict word is out of the message stream entirely — it is now stated once for the conversation and printed again only where a message departs from it. Everything else here is IRCv3 rendered as interface: batches, message tags, standard replies, account and away notifications, and what capability negotiation actually bought.

11

The band and the exception

A verdict repeated on every message is a fact about the conversation printed as though it were a fact about each line. So state it once, where it starts, and print it again only where a message departs from it. In an encrypted channel the encrypted messages carry no word at all — and the plaintext one carries a word you cannot miss.

previous study — one word per run
sable10:58 encrypted
the removal path is merged, the tag is unblocked
kade10:59 encrypted
did the pacing regression get folded in
phrack11:00 encrypted
yes, same branch
tsutomu11:02 plaintext
is the meeting still at 4
Better than one word per line, but the word encrypted is still doing nothing three times before it does something once. The eye has already stopped reading that column by the time plaintext arrives in it.

Three messages carry no verdict word, because the band above them already said it and nothing changed. The fourth carries one, in a bordered pill, with its consequence in a sentence. It is the only word of its kind on the screen.

where the word still lives

Removing it from the stream is only defensible if it is unremovable everywhere else. Four places, and all four are outside anything a theme, script or plugin can reach:

bandonce, at the point the conversation's verdict starts or changes
sticky headeralways, naming the state at the top edge of the viewport
status baralways, with the metadata truth and transport beside it
the messageon hover, focus, selection, and in Copy with verdict
kade10:59 encrypted · megolm session 4f21 · 9 recipients
did the pacing regression get folded in
Focused. The per-message answer is longer than the gutter could ever have been, because it is no longer competing for width with the conversation.

the honest argument with §1

§1 says a verdict must be "unforgeable, always present, and readable without a legend". Always present is the clause under strain, and the reading that survives is present on the screen rather than present on every row: no viewport of this design lacks the word, because the sticky header cannot scroll away.

Readable without a legend gets stronger, not weaker. A screenshot of the old pane shows a column of identical words that a reader must know how to interpret. A screenshot of this one shows encrypted at the top and a single pill reading plaintext beside the line it applies to.

Unforgeable is unchanged and gets a new problem to solve: message text can now sit where a verdict is not, so a sender could type something that reads like an exception pill. The pill therefore lives in the block header, outside the body's text flow, in a face and a colour no message body is ever rendered in — the same structural argument that keeps the status bar unforgeable today.

the case this must not break

A channel where the verdict alternates message to message — half the room holds keys and half does not — would produce a pill on every other line, which is the old problem wearing a new shape. The rule that prevents it: when exceptions exceed a third of the visible messages, the band restates itself as mixed and every message carries its word again. The design degrades back to the honest, noisy rendering exactly when the conversation is genuinely mixed, and never before.

batch — grouping the client is allowed to do

Every other grouping on these pages is the client guessing. A batch is the server saying these lines belong together and here is why. That makes it the only collapse in the design that carries no risk of hiding something the reader needed — and it is the single highest-value readability capability IRCv3 offers.

A msgid is what makes a message referenceable, and everything interesting hangs off that: a reply that points at a line, a reaction that attaches to one, an edit that replaces one. The account tag is the quiet one, and for this client it is the most important of the four.

+draft/react

Reactions carry names, not just counts

A count alone is a popularity metric. The line under the chips names who reacted, because in a nine-person channel that is the actual information — and it is one line, not a hover.

+draft/reply

A reply quotes one line, at one third weight

The excerpt is truncated, muted and non-interactive. It orients the reader without competing with the reply itself, and it is drawn from the referenced msgid rather than from text the sender typed.

edit / redact

An edit says it edited

Never a silent replacement. The marker states the time and that the original is retained locally — a client that lets a sender rewrite history invisibly has handed them a capability the protocol did not.

account

The nick is a label; the account is the identity

ircx already knows this — §5.6 exists because a nick returns to the pool when its holder disconnects. The account tag makes it renderable on every message, so the mismatch above is caught the moment it speaks rather than the moment you type /allow.

Why the account block is attention and not refused. Nothing was blocked — phrack2 sent a message and you read it. What is wrong is your assumption, not the transmission, and colouring it refused would be the alarm-fatigue mistake §2 warns about. The band names the change, the pill names the state, and the two commands that resolve it are on the line. No one-click "trust anyway".

Standard replies — the end of numerics as interface

FAIL, WARN and NOTE carry a command, a machine-readable code and a human sentence. That is exactly the shape handoff §7 already demands of every error in this client — what happened and what to do, one sentence each — so the rendering is almost written for us. The only addition ircx makes is the consequence line.

numerics
09:41--:libera.chat 421 you REGISTER :Unknown command
09:41--:libera.chat 902 you :You must use a nick assigned to you
09:42--:libera.chat 486 you :You must be identified
09:42--:libera.chat 435 you #ircd-dev :Cannot change nickname while banned
Four failures, four numbers, no indication of which command failed or whether anything can be done. A client can special-case the ones it knows and is stuck printing the raw line for the rest.
standard replies
failREGISTER ACCOUNT_EXISTS09:41
The account $sable is already registered.
Nothing was sent. Use /msg NickServ identify if it is yours, or pick another account name.
warn* MULTILINE_MAX_BYTES09:44
Your message was longer than this server accepts in one batch.
It was sent as four separate messages. A reaction can only attach to the first of them. Your text is unchanged in the buffer.
noteCHATHISTORY MAX_MESSAGES_EXCEEDED09:45
The server returned 100 of the 400 messages requested.
Older lines are not lost; scroll up to ask for the next block.
Severity as a word, the command it belongs to, the code a script can match on, and a sentence a person can act on. Three registers, three left borders, no numbers to look up.
The unknown-code case is the one that matters. A client will always meet a code it has no copy for. The rendering degrades to severity + command + code + whatever sentence the server sent, and the consequence line becomes a stated absence: ircx has no additional guidance for this code. That is §2's "absences are stated, not implied" — a blank space would read as "nothing to worry about".
And severity is the server's word, not ours. ircx does not promote a WARN to a refusal block or demote a FAIL to a note. The three registers map to the three left-border weights and nothing re-decides them — the same reason a frontend never computes a verdict.
Why the severity is lower case and the code is not. §7 forbids capitals for emphasis, and §8 gives the reason: a screen reader spells them out, so FAIL is read aloud as four letters. The severity is interface chrome, so it is set as a word. The code is an opaque machine identifier a person may need to paste into a search, so it is reproduced verbatim, in monospace, inside a bordered chip that marks it as quoted rather than written. The raw wire token is in the protocol log either way.

Three grades of grouping, and how sure the client is

Studies 04 and 12 drew grouping as a binary: a protocol reply got a solid connector, an inferred one got a dotted one. That is one grade too few. A bracketed topic the sender typed is a fact. IRC's nick: addressing convention is near-certain. Everything else is a guess. Rendering all three at the same strength is the interface claiming to know more than it does.

The last message is in no group, and that has to look like nothing. Not a fourth style, not an "ungrouped" header — a message that belongs to nothing is the ordinary case and gets the ordinary rendering. A style for it would make the absence of a claim look like a claim.

three strengths

2px solid · a name
Declared — the sender said so

Someone typed [parser] at the head of a line. The client strips the bracket and uses it as the group name. The only grade that gets a name, because it is the only one where a human named it — and in the live stream the name is the whole label.

2px solid · nothing
Addressed — the convention

A leading nick: or nick, is thirty years of practice and is near-certain. Solid, because it is a fact about the text. In the live stream it carries no label at all — both participants are already in the blocks below, in their own colours, so a caption naming them says nothing new.

2px dashed · a dismiss chip
Guessed — the client's idea

Timing and participants and nothing else. Dashed, and the only one of the three carrying a dismiss affordance. The affordance is the label — only a guess can be undone, so an offer to undo tells the reader the client did this, with no sentence explaining the heuristic.

Stroke does the ranking, not hue. Certainty is ordinal — it has a direction — and hue has none, so three hues would read as three categories a reader has to memorise. Solid versus dashed reads as fact versus guess at a glance, in greyscale, in a screenshot, and to anyone with any colour vision deficiency.
Once the labels went, the weights could merge. Declared and addressed are both facts and now share one weight; the name is what separates them, which is exactly as visible as it needs to be. The distinction that has to be unmissable is fact versus guess, and that is solid versus dashed.

The rules that keep it honest

precedenceDeclared beats addressed beats guessed, and a message is in at most one group. The client never guesses at messages it could have grouped from a fact — a guess is what is left over, not a competing opinion.
dismissableOnly the guess. Dismissing it ungroups those messages in place: nothing moves, nothing is hidden, the timestamps do not change, and the client does not re-propose that same group. It is a correction of one instance, not a preference.
never hidesNo grade reorders, filters, collapses or hides anything. A group is a label and an indent. The stream stays chronological, so a reader who ignores the grouping entirely loses nothing at all.
never dashedThe verdict spine inside a block is never dashed at any strength. Dashed can only ever mean "the client grouped this" — it can never be read as "the client is unsure about the security of this". A verdict has no grades.
a name is not chromeA declared name is text the sender typed. It renders in the message face and in ordinary ink, never in the monospace chrome register and never in the security palette — so someone who types [encrypted] as a topic gets a group called encrypted, in body ink, and nothing that resembles a verdict.
plaintext tooGrouping is a readability feature and has nothing to do with encryption, so all three grades appear in plaintext channels — where they are needed most. That creates the one real collision in this design: in an all-plaintext channel the verdict spine is neutral grey and so is the group rule, two vertical lines 14px apart. What separates them is structural rather than tonal — a group rule always has a label above it and spans the whole group; a spine never has one and spans a single author block. If that proves too subtle in use, the fix is to drop the spine entirely wherever a conversation's verdict is uniform, since the band has already said it and a muted bar beside every message is the same repetition the band was built to remove.
almost nothingIn compact and comfortable density a declared group shows its name and nothing else — not the count, not the word declared. An addressed group shows nothing. A guessed group shows only the dismiss chip, positioned so it costs no vertical space. Full provenance appears on hover or focus, and returns as a header in read density, where someone catching up on four hundred messages does want to know what the client invented.
a floorA guess needs a minimum size and a minimum confidence, or it is not drawn. Two adjacent messages from two people is not a topic, and a dashed rule around it costs more attention than the grouping is worth.

Colour on the rule: which strand, not how sure

In a channel whose verdict is uniform — every public channel, all the time — the per-message spine is carrying nothing. The band said plaintext once at the top and the status bar is still saying it. So the spine goes, the group rule becomes the only vertical line, and its hue is free to answer the question a busy room actually raises: which conversation is this one.

and it still holds in an encrypted channel

The spine only disappears where the verdict is uniform. what this costs, stated plainly

Dropping the spine takes away the author block's left edge. With the monogram chips gone too, an ungrouped message in a uniform channel is now held together by spacing and its header line alone. Inside a group the rule does that job; outside one, nothing does.

That is survivable — an ungrouped message is usually a single line, and a block edge around one line is decoration. But it is the load-bearing assumption of this variant, and if ungrouped multi-line messages turn out to read as loose, the answer is to keep a neutral spine at low weight everywhere and accept the two-grey-rules problem instead. Worth finding out in a real channel rather than here.

one correction to the reference sketch

The sketch this came from renders the declared group's header in green. That green is #1D9E75's neighbourhood — the protected hue from handoff §3.1. A grouping claim rendered in the colour that means "encrypted, all devices verified" is the same collision as finding 1, arriving from a different direction.

So certainty spends no hue at all. The three grades live entirely in the neutral ink ramp and in stroke weight and style. Warm stays reserved for security, cool stays reserved for identity, and grouping — which is the client talking about itself — gets neither.

What this replaces. The solid/dotted strand connector in studies 04 and 12 was the same idea with a grade missing and no name for the basis. This supersedes it: a connector said that these two are linked, a group header says why the client thinks so, and the second is the sentence a reader can actually disagree with.

And it composes with the band. Grouping is a neutral rule outside the block; the verdict is a coloured spine inside it. Two vertical lines, never confusable: one is never coloured, the other is never dashed, and they are 14px apart. In the pane above, the encrypted band at the top covers all four groups without any of them restating it.

The input line answers before you type. Away is a fact about whether a message will be read, so it belongs where you decide to send one — next to the lock state, in the same slot, in the muted register rather than the warning one. Being away is not a problem.

an account change is a security event

A nick returning to the pool is the mechanism handoff §5.6 already guards against at /allow time. With account-notify and extended-join, ircx can catch it at the moment it happens instead.

account changed at 14:09, mid-conversation

The nick phrack now maps to $phrack2, not $phrack.

Quiet when expected, loud when changed. An ordinary login — nyx identifying as $nyx, the account it always has — produces nothing at all. The account chip on the block simply starts being there. Only a change from a known mapping produces this.
Rendered as a standard reply on purpose. ircx generates this one itself, so it uses the same shape a server-sent WARN would, with ircx in the command slot. A reader should not have to learn two vocabularies for the same severity, and the source is named rather than hidden.

/caps — what negotiation actually bought

Everything in these studies is conditional on a capability. When one is missing the feature it powers does not silently vanish — the screen says which feature, and what the absence costs. This is handoff §5.4 applied to capabilities instead of transport: print the good answers and the weak ones in the same number of words.

Delta, continued

Additions to the handoff table in study 10. §1 is still untouched, but §4.1 now needs a rewrite rather than a replacement, and that is the change worth arguing about before any of it is built.

sectionstatuswhat changes
§1 the ruleclarify "Always present" is read as present in every viewport, not on every row. The sticky header cannot scroll away, so no screenshot of the client lacks the word. This is the load-bearing claim of studies 11–18 and it should be settled explicitly rather than assumed.
§4.1 gutterrewrite Band, spine, exception, sticky header. The conversation's verdict is stated at every change; a message carries its own word only where it departs from the band. Above one third exceptions in view, the band restates as mixed and every message is labelled again.
new §4.7add Batches. A batch is the only collapse the client may perform on the server's authority. A netsplit summary must state whether anyone who left held a key. A chathistory batch carries its own band, a dashed edge, and both clocks.
new §4.8add Standard replies. Severity is the server's word and is never promoted or demoted. An unknown code renders with a stated absence rather than a blank consequence. ircx's own warnings use the same shape with ircx in the command slot.
new §4.9add Reactions and the metadata clause. If reactions are rendered, the status bar names them in what the server sees. A reaction on a plaintext line is public; on an encrypted line it is sealed in the same session, and the two are never drawn the same way.
§5.x newadd /caps. Enabled and absent capabilities in one list, each absence followed by the feature it disables and what the reader will observe instead. Ends with the what is not here section §5.8 already requires elsewhere.
new §4.10add Grouping provenance. Three grades — declared, addressed by name, guessed — ranked by stroke weight and style rather than hue, since certainty is ordinal and hue is not. Only the guess is dismissable. No grade reorders, filters or hides. The verdict spine is never dashed at any strength, so dashed can only ever mean "the client grouped this".
§4.6 strandssuperseded Replaced by §4.10. The solid/dotted connector was the same idea with one grade missing and no name for the basis. A group header states why the client thinks the messages belong together, which is a sentence the reader can disagree with.
§4.2 member listextend Away is a roster state and an input-line state, never a message. At public-channel scale the roster sorts by operators, then recent speakers, then a sentence — the filter is the completeness route, per §6.
Still open. Whether the one-third exception threshold is the right trigger, or whether mixed should be a per-buffer setting the user can pin. Whether an account chip belongs on every message or only on the first block from that account in a session. And whether encrypted reactions are worth the metadata they add — a sealed reaction still tells the server that someone reacted to something, which is a fact the plaintext case does not make worse.
