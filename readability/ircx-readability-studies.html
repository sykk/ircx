ircx / readability studies
superseded in part

Studies 01–10 are the argument that produced revision 2 of the handoff, and three things in them were replaced by what came after. The verdict word is no longer printed once per author run — it is stated in a band and again only on exceptions (handoff §4.1). The sticky "at the top of this view" header is deleted. The spine is drawn only where a message departs from the band. For the current rendering see ircx-live-studies.html and the handoff; the two colour findings and everything about grouping, measure, presence and typography here are still current.

Readability was never a font size problem.

Every mockup below is live: switch theme and density in the bar above and the client re-renders. Nine studies, each attacking one specific way the current ircx window is hard to read — without touching the rule that a security verdict is unforgeable, always present, and readable without a legend.

00

What people actually mean by "hard to read"

Nobody complains that IRC text is too small. They complain about six things that all look like one thing. Naming them separately is what makes them fixable.

flat weight

Everything is the same size

A sentence from a person, ChanServ announcing a mode change, and a refusal from the client all render at 11.5px in the same face. The eye gets no hierarchy, so it reads every line or none.

no grouping

No paragraph, no author block

Six consecutive lines from one person are six independent rows, each re-stating a timestamp and a nick. The repetition costs more attention than the content.

noise

Presence churn outranks speech

Joins, parts, quits, nick changes and mode flips are events the server emits, not things anybody said. They currently occupy the same column, weight and vertical space as speech.

interleaving

Three conversations, one column

A busy channel is several strands braided together. Without any reply structure the reader does the de-braiding, every time, from scratch.

measure

Lines run to the window edge

Maximised on a wide display, a message can run past 200 characters. Comfortable reading measure is 45–85. The eye loses the return sweep and re-reads the same line.

repetition

The verdict repeats itself away

The word encrypted printed on forty consecutive rows is forty chances to learn to skip it. §2 of the handoff already names this failure — "confirmations that fire every time get trained through" — and the gutter is doing it.

02

The verdict spine

The handoff requires a verdict to be unforgeable, always present, and readable without a legend. It does not require it to be printed on every line, and printing it on every line is what makes it invisible. The spine keeps all three properties and drops the repetition.

anatomy — the word prints at every change
at the top of this view: encrypted
sable11:02encrypted
rekey landed
two more devices to go
phrack11:04encrypted
good. I will take the removal path
nyx11:05unverified
from my laptop — that device is not verified yet
tsutomu11:12plaintext
is the meeting still at 4
wren11:14refused
signed by a device whose key was revoked — not shown
bitwise11:15undecryptable
sent before you had the session key
All five words, one column. The vocabulary in §4.1 is exhaustive and closed: encrypted, unverified, plaintext, refused, undecryptable. Every one of them fits on the header line at full length in both themes at every density on this page.

five properties, all preserved

Always present. The sticky header names the verdict of whatever run is at the top edge of the viewport. Scroll anywhere, screenshot anything: the word is in the frame.

Never truncated. The word sits on the block header line, which has the whole measure to spend. Nothing about it is width-negotiated any more, so the collapse ladder in §4.1 stops being needed.

Words, not only colour. Unchanged. The bar is redundant reinforcement of a word that is already there.

A change is loud. A verdict change ends the run. Two people with the same verdict group; the same person across a verdict change does not. Structure now carries the security event.

Per line on demand. Hovering, focusing or selecting any single line still names its own verdict, and Copy with verdict is unaffected.

The honest cost. A run of forty encrypted messages shows the word twice — once at its head, once in the sticky header. If that is judged too few for §1, the fallback is to re-print at every author change rather than every line, which is roughly a fifth of today's repetition and still leaves the word on screen at all times.

finding 1 — the nick palette collides with the security palette

In 01-main-window.html, sable is drawn in #1D9E75 and tsutomu in #BA7517. Those are not near the security palette, they are the security palette: §3.1 protected and attention exactly. A hash-assigned nick colour can therefore render a person in the colour that means "encrypted, all devices verified" while their message is plaintext — which is what happened to tsutomu in that very mockup.

Proposed rule: warm hues are reserved for security. Identity lives in the cool half of the wheel, hues 180–350, and the nick palette is generated inside that band rather than picked. Ten hues, 17° apart, none within 20° of a security hue.

finding 2 — the dark security palette does not meet AA on the project's own surfaces

§8 states the §3.1 values "meet WCAG AA against both the light and dark defaults". Measured against the dark surfaces in tokens.css, they do not. The mockups themselves render the message pane on --surface-2, where every one of the three fails.

Presence is weather, not speech

Joins, parts, quits, nick changes and mode flips are things the server said. They belong in a different register from things a person said. The digest collapses them into one line of prose — except the parts with security consequences, which stay loud.

04

Strands — de-braiding without inventing threads

IRC has no threads and ircx should not pretend otherwise. But a client can notice that phrack addressed nyx by name eleven seconds after nyx spoke, and it can indent that. Click a strand to dim the others; nothing is ever hidden, and the state is not saved.

at the top of this view: encrypted
nyx14:02encrypted
rekey on member removal is landing tonight, anything left to review
tsutomu14:02encrypted
unrelated: are we still tagging 0.9 on friday
phrack14:03encrypted
nyx — rekey on member removal is landing tonight…
the removal path yes. the backfill question is still open
sable14:03encrypted
tsutomu friday still holds, the tag is not blocked on the rekey work
nyx14:04encrypted
phrack then let us ship removal and leave backfill for phase 12
tsutomu14:05encrypted
good. I will write the notes
Solid connector, dotted connector. A solid line and a quoted excerpt mean the sender's client sent a real reply reference over message-tags — a protocol fact. A dotted line means ircx inferred the link from a leading nick mention inside a short window — a guess. The two are never drawn the same way, because a heuristic that looks like a fact is the kind of small lie this project otherwise refuses to tell.
Where a channel has no reply capability at all, strands still work from mentions, and the buffer says so once in its header rather than per message: no message-tags here — strands are inferred from mentions. That is §2's "say what an absence costs", applied to a reading feature rather than a security one.

06

Nick colour, and how much work it is allowed to do

Twelve arbitrary hues hashed from a nickname is the traditional approach and it fails two ways: the hues collide with the security palette, and the contrast is unspecified. What it does not fail is the usual accessibility objection — and the reason is worth stating, because it also decides how much work the colour is allowed to do.

the generated band

Ten hues, evenly spaced across 186–339°, each solved numerically for the lowest lightness that clears 5:1 against the pane. Not chosen by eye and not stored as literals — regenerated when the pane colour changes, which is what makes a user theme safe rather than merely allowed.

the nickname is the carrier; colour is reinforcement

There is no avatar in IRC and this design does not invent one. The identifier is a string, it is written out in full on every block header, and it is the thing a person actually reads. So colour here is never load-bearing — unlike an initial-in-a-circle interface, where removing colour leaves two letters and a shape.

Turn colour off entirely, or hand the window to someone with deuteranopia, and nothing is lost but speed:

sable11:02encrypted
colour removed entirely — the block still reads
nyx11:03unverified
and the verdict is still a word, in the header, untruncated
Reserved. The security palette hues — 163° green, 38° amber, 1° red — are excluded from the generated band with a 20° margin, so no nickname can ever be drawn in a colour that carries a security claim.
Which is why the palette's only job is separation. It is not carrying identity and it is not carrying security, so the contrast floor exists for legibility rather than for recognition, and ten hues is plenty. If a channel ever has more than ten active speakers, two of them share a colour and nothing breaks — their names are still different.
07

Most of what IRC carries is not prose

URLs, pastes, tracebacks, fingerprints, file offers. Setting them in the same 11.5px monospace run as conversation is what produces the wall. The rule here is prose gets the text face, data keeps monospace — and anything with a shape gets that shape.

URLs become domain-forward chips. The host is what a reader is deciding on, so the host is what is legible; the path is elided, and the full target is in the title and the status bar on focus. A 340-character tracking URL stops destroying the paragraph it sits in.
Pastes get a container, not a flood. Eighteen lines of protocol log become a bounded, scrollable block with a stated line count. It is still monospace, still selectable, still complete — it just stops being eighteen equal-weight rows in the conversation.
And fingerprints keep their grouping. Space-separated groups of four in monospace, per handoff §5.8 — because that is the shape a human compares against a phone screen. This is the case where monospace is not a habit but a requirement, which is exactly why spending it on ordinary sentences devalues it.
08

The seam — where you stopped reading

Every client draws an unread line. Almost none of them tell you the size of the thing you are about to read, and none of them tell you whether the security picture changed while you were gone — which for this client is the more useful sentence.

at the top of this view: encrypted
sable08:51encrypted
I will pick this up in the morning
41 messages, 4 people, 9 hours  ·  2 mention you  ·  the verdict changed twice
nyx02:14unverified
joined from a new laptop overnight, that device is not verified
phrack07:40encrypted
you the removal patch is ready whenever you are
Why the third clause matters. "The verdict changed twice" is the only part of a catch-up summary that a reader cannot reconstruct by skimming, because the verdict of a message you skip is a verdict you never saw. Naming it at the seam is the one place a summary can carry security information without ever summarising away a verdict — it points at the changes, it does not replace them.

10

What this would change in the handoff

Section 1 is untouched and every proposal above was checked against it. Three sections would need amending, two of them because they are currently wrong rather than because this design disagrees with them.

sectionstatuswhat changes
§1 the ruleunchanged Verdicts stay words, untruncated, unforgeable, unthemeable. The spine is redundant reinforcement of a word, exactly as colour is today. Nothing here makes a verdict shorter, quieter, colour-only or optional.
§3.1 palettescorrection Dark security values are replaced with #1FAF86, #CF8D1A, #E77B79. The current ones fail AA on the project's own dark surfaces, which makes §8's claim untrue as written. Light values are unchanged.
§3.1 nick palettecorrection The nick palette is constrained to hues 180–350 and generated, not listed. Today it can, and in the reference mockup does, assign a person the exact colour of a security verdict.
§3.2 typeamend "Monospace throughout" becomes semantic. Prose gets the text face; nicks, channels, commands, verdicts, fingerprints, hostmasks, filenames and pasted content keep monospace. This is the largest departure on this page and the one most worth arguing about — it is also the single change that does the most for readability, because it stops monospace from meaning nothing by making it mean something.
§4.1 gutterreplaced The fixed-width right-hand column becomes a left spine plus a per-run word and a sticky viewport header. The yield-order ladder is deleted rather than rewritten: nothing about the verdict is width-negotiated any more, so there is no narrowing case left to specify. This also closes open question §10.5.
§4.2 member listunchanged The two-group split, the enumerate/count asymmetry, and its absence in plaintext conversations are all preserved.
§4.3 refusalunchanged Same two lines, same square corners, same promise that the text is still in the input line, same absence of a one-click override.
§5.1 main windowamend The message pane gains a reading measure and a density setting. Fixed column widths become minimums; the pane stops stretching text to the window width.
new §4.5add Presence digest. An event may be digested only if it changes nothing about who can read the conversation. Events that do are named in the digest's first clause in attention colour and have no setting that hides them.
new §4.6add Strands. A protocol reply and an inferred one are drawn differently — solid connector with a quoted excerpt versus dotted connector — and a strand focus dims, never hides, and is never persisted.
Two things deliberately not proposed. No message actions were added to a context menu, because §4.4's argument that a one-click menu is the wrong home for anything dangerous applies equally to anything new. And no summary, digest or catch-up view ever states a verdict on behalf of messages it collapsed — the seam counts the changes and points at them, and the messages still carry their own.
